A Methods section is a recipe. Cook from one, then reverse one.

Part 1: write the Methods for a Stroop task, then build exactly that task from the Week 2 reaction-time page and run it on yourself. Part 2: start from a published result, guess the Methods that produced it, check against the paper, and rerun the authors' own data.

The task

Stroop: name the ink, not the word

BLUE in red ink is slower to name than RED in red ink. The slowdown (incongruent minus congruent RT) is the Stroop effect, one of the most replicated results in psychology. The task is a small edit of the page you built in Week 2.

The paper

Hedge, Powell & Sumner (2018)

“The reliability paradox: Why robust cognitive tasks do not produce reliable individual differences.” Behavior Research Methods, 50(3), 1166–1186. doi:10.3758/s13428-017-0935-1. Open data on OSF. Everyone shows a Stroop effect, yet the same person's effect three weeks later is only partly predictable. Do not open the PDF until Part 2.

Part 2 · backward · about 60 minutes

From Hedge's result back to Hedge's Methods

Here is what the paper reports for the Stroop task. Every participant was tested twice. Reliability is an intraclass correlation (ICC): 1 means people kept exactly the same rank order from session 1 to session 2, 0 means no relation.

  1. Guess first. Under My guess in the README, write the Methods you think produced this: who, how many trials, which colours and keys, how long between sessions, which trials and which people were excluded. Commit.
  2. Reveal. Open the open-access PDF and read the Stroop Methods (or open the box below). Under What I missed, list three details you got wrong or never thought of, and whether each could change the result.
  3. Reanalyse. In a terminal in week07-methods/: 
    python3 analyze_stroop.py --mine stroop-week07.csv
    It downloads the tidied Hedge data (every trial, both studies, both sessions), applies their exclusion rules, and prints the group effect, the session 1 vs 2 correlation, and where your own effect falls. Paste output/numbers.md into the README.
  4. Run it again with --rule course (our 150–2500 ms rule instead of theirs). What changed? One sentence on why exclusion rules belong in a Methods section, written in numbers.
Reveal: Hedge et al.'s Stroop Methods, in brief
  • Participants. Undergraduates. Study 1: 50 (ages 18–21); Study 2: 62 (ages 18–47). Two 90-minute sessions, three weeks apart, same time of day. People who did not return were dropped, as was anyone below 60% accuracy in either session.
  • Materials. PsychoPy. Words in Arial, size 70, centred. Ink red, blue, green, or yellow, answered with Z, X, N, M.
  • Conditions. Congruent (word matches ink), incongruent (word names another response colour), and neutral (lot, ship, cross, advice, matched for length and frequency).
  • Procedure. Word stays until a response; 750 ms between trials. 240 trials per condition, 720 in all, randomly intermixed, in five blocks of about 4 minutes with feedback on mean RT and errors after each block.
  • Data handling. RT cost = incongruent − congruent. RTs under 100 ms, or more than three median absolute deviations from the person's median, were left out.

Notice what is still ambiguous. Is that median taken per condition or across all trials? The script picks one reading and says so. And the script keeps 105 people where the paper analysed 103 (47 + 56): the 60% rule alone does not catch every person they dropped. What else would the Methods need to say?
